# Measuring strength

Everything this project claims after today is a number produced by
`chess match`. This is what those numbers mean, what they cannot mean, and why
the harness is built the way it is.

Top-layer pointers: `STATE.md` for the current number, `CHECKPOINTS.md` for the
kept versions, `LEDGER.md` for the experiments.

---

## 1. A match measures a difference, not a strength

A match between two engines estimates one quantity: the expected score of A
against B. That maps to an Elo difference by the logistic model

    score = 1 / (1 + 10^(-elo/400))

and nothing else. It says nothing about how strong either engine is in
absolute terms. Absolute strength requires an opponent whose rating is already
known on some published scale — see §5.

## 2. Pairs, not games

Games are played two at a time: the same opening, colours reversed, and the
**pair** is the unit of statistics.

The reason is variance, not tidiness. A game's result is driven by three
things: the opening, the colour, and the difference in engine strength. Only
the third is being measured; the other two are noise added to every sample.
Playing both colours from the same opening differences them out — a pair where
both engines win with white tells you the opening favoured white, and
contributes *nothing* to the estimated difference, which is exactly correct.

Concretely the pair takes one of five values (0, ½, 1, 1½, 2 points to A), and
the estimator uses the sample variance of those. This is the pentanomial model
fishtest uses. Treating paired games as independent (the trinomial model)
understates the correlation and produces error bars that are too narrow — the
classic way to convince yourself a patch works when it does not. It is worth
roughly 25-30% fewer games to a decision, for free.

## 3. SPRT: when to stop

A fixed-length match is the wrong instrument. At the effect sizes an engine
change produces, 100 games has a 95% interval of roughly ±70 Elo — it cannot
distinguish a good patch from a harmful one, and no amount of staring at the
W-L-D helps.

The sequential probability ratio test instead fixes the *error rates* and lets
the length float. With H0 "worth `elo0`" and H1 "worth `elo1`", it accumulates

    LLR = n (s1 - s0)(2 s̄ - s0 - s1) / (2 σ²)

over pairs and stops when it crosses `log(β/(1-α))` or `log((1-β)/α)`. For a
real effect this is typically 2-5x fewer games than a fixed match of the same
power, and — more valuable — it stops *early* on a regression instead of
burning the full budget proving what the first fifty games suggested.

**Small samples.** The pair variance is estimated with one pseudo-pair added
to each of the five outcomes — a Dirichlet(1) prior. Without it, a match that
happens to be all draws has zero observed variance, the normal approximation
claims infinite precision, and the test decides instantly on no evidence. A
hard floor also fixes that but makes the strength of the conclusion depend on
an arbitrary constant. The smoothing costs about 2% of the variance estimate at
200 pairs and degrades smoothly. The *mean* is left raw: shrinking it would
pull an anchored rating towards the opponent's, which is exactly the number we
are trying to measure.

**Bounds.** The default gate here is SPRT(0, 10) at α=β=0.05, not the
SPRT(0, 5) that Stockfish uses. That is a deliberate consequence of the
project's bet: we are not running the +1 Elo patch grind, we are looking for
structural changes worth tens of Elo, and a tighter H1 would spend thousands of
games resolving effects we are not trying to find. If a change is worth +3 Elo
this gate will report it as inconclusive. That is the correct answer for us.

## 4. What the harness refuses to do

**Adjudication is two-sided.** A game is called as won only when *both*
engines' scores agree on the margin for 8 consecutive plies, and drawn only
when both report near-zero for 16 plies after move 40. One-sided adjudication —
what cutechess does by default — lets the engine under test decide its own
games, which means an eval bug can manufacture wins for itself. The cost is a
few extra plies per game; the benefit is that the measurement cannot be
corrupted by the thing being measured.

**Broken engines lose loudly.** A timeout, a crash, or an illegal move ends the
game as a loss and prints the reason, and the final report says how many games
ended that way. A harness that silently absorbs engine errors into the score
will happily report that your latest patch is worth -40 Elo when what actually
happened is that it segfaults once every fifty games. (Which is a real
regression — but a different one, and you want to be told which.)

**The book is not ours.** 44 opening lines from theory, 4-8 plies, spanning
open/closed/semi-open/flank. Deliberately *not* generated by our own engine: a
book of "positions our eval scores near zero" would select for openings that
suit our eval, which is the thing being measured. Every line is checked legal
by a unit test, because a typo in the book does not crash — it quietly
corrupts a fraction of the games.

## 5. Turning a difference into a rating

`tools/ladder.sh` builds open-source engines at the exact versions CCRL has
rated, plays a gauntlet, and combines the results:

| opponent | CCRL Blitz | language |
|---|---|---|
| BBC 1.1 | 2019 | C |
| Goldfish 2.1.1 | 2252 | Rust |
| Cinnamon 2.4 | 2326 | C++ |
| Tantabus 2.0.0 | 2555 | Rust |
| Blunder 8.5.5 | 2664 | Go |

Each match gives `rating_i = ccrl_i + diff_i`, and these are combined by
inverse-variance weighting, propagating both our statistical error and CCRL's
published interval on the opponent. The script also reports χ²/dof across the
opponents: if the per-opponent estimates disagree by more than their error
bars, the single-number model is not fitting and the combination should be
distrusted — usually a style mismatch against one opponent.

Two details that are not decoration:

* **Exact rated tags.** Each opponent is checked out at the tag CCRL tested.
  Building master gives a binary with no published rating, which is the same as
  having no anchor.
* **Ratings are fetched, not typed.** They come from the live CCRL Blitz list,
  matched on the exact engine name string, with the list's own computation date
  recorded. A rating typed from memory is a silent systematic error in every
  result that ever cites it.

## 6. What the absolute number does *not* account for

Hardware cancels: both engines run on this box, so the head-to-head difference
is clean. What does not cancel is that CCRL's rating of the opponent was earned
under different conditions:

| CCRL Blitz | here |
|---|---|
| 12-move opening book | 4-8 ply book |
| 6-piece tablebases | none |
| 2'+1" scaled to an i7-4770K | 10+0.1 |
| their whole opponent pool | five engines |

The time control is the largest of these. A shorter control favours engines
whose strength is in raw speed over engines whose strength is in search depth,
and our anchor runs roughly 6-8x faster than the rated condition. The
statistical interval the script prints does not cover any of this.

**So quote it as a scale, not a certificate:** "about X on the CCRL Blitz
scale", with the interval, the time control and the list date attached. A
confirmation run at a longer control is the obvious follow-up, and is cheap in
everything except wall time.

## 7. The one asymmetry worth knowing about

The runner can play the engine under test in-process (fast, no pipe) or as a
subprocess. These are different code paths, so a match between them could carry
a systematic bias.

Measured: 400 games at 100ms/move, the same build against itself, in-process
vs subprocess, gave **+4.3 Elo [-18, +27]**. Consistent with zero, but only
resolved to about ±22 — which is the same order as the effects we care about.

So the checkpoint gate runs **both sides as subprocesses**, where the
asymmetry cancels exactly by construction rather than being assumed small. The
in-process player stays for cheap `Params` experiments, where both sides are
in-process and it cancels there too.
